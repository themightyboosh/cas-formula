Slide 1: The Problem You're Living
"Something's happening inside me, and I can't make sense of it."
You know the feeling. Something's off. You're reactive, or shut down, or spinning. You can tell something's happening in your body—but when someone asks "how are you feeling?" you freeze. You don't have the words. Or worse, the words you have don't capture it.
The Pain Points
"I don't know what I'm feeling."
Something's happening, but you can't name it. Emotional confusion. A blur where clarity should be.
"I know I'm upset, but I don't know why I react the way I do."
You watch yourself do the thing—shut down, lash out, overfunction, disappear—and you don't understand why.
"I keep doing the same thing and it never works."
Same fights. Same withdrawals. Same collapses. You're stuck in a loop you can't see clearly enough to exit.
"I feel like no one understands me—including myself."
Your inner experience feels invisible. Untranslatable. Like you're speaking a language no one else knows.
"My emotions happen TO me."
No agency. Weather without a weather report. You're at the mercy of something you can't see.
Slide 2: Why Nothing Has Worked
Current tools ask you to do the very thing you can't do.
The Vocabulary Trap
Every emotion app, every therapy intake, every well-meaning friend asks the same thing: "How do you feel?" Then they hand you a list of words—angry, sad, anxious, afraid—and ask you to pick one.
But that's the problem. If you had the words, you wouldn't need the tool.
What These Tools Require
• Translate your bodily experience into cognitive labels
• Evaluate a proposition ("I feel angry") against your felt sense
• Work from the top down—from language to body
• Already know what you're trying to discover
For anyone who struggles to name emotions—and that's most of us, most of the time—these tools ask you to guess. You're thinking about what you might be feeling, rather than noticing what's already happening.
The Description Problem
Even when these tools work, they only tell you WHAT you're feeling. "You're anxious." Okay. Now what?
They don't tell you WHY you handle it the way you do. They don't reveal the automatic pattern you've been running for decades. They describe the weather without explaining the terrain.
Slide 3: What We Discovered
Your body already knows. You don't have to find the words.
The Science
Cognitive linguistics research (Lakoff, Johnson) reveals something profound: we don't just DESCRIBE emotions with metaphors—we THINK and EXPERIENCE them through embodied metaphorical structures.
When you say "I'm sinking," that's not decoration. That's how your body-mind is actually organizing the experience. The metaphor isn't describing the feeling—it IS the feeling's cognitive form.
The Breakthrough
We don't ask you to name what you feel.
We let your body recognize itself.
Instead of "Rate how angry you feel," we present:
"Blood boiling, pressure building, heat rising, ready to explode"
You don't translate. You recognize. Your body says "yes, that's me" before your mind can interfere.
Why This Works
Embodied metaphors bypass the cognitive-labeling layer entirely. You don't need to know the word "shame" to recognize "want to disappear, feeling small, shrinking." The structure is already there—we just help you see it.

Slide 6: How It Works
A 3-minute journey from confusion to clarity
Step 1: Arrive (30 seconds)
One breath. Drop from your head into your body. Name what's on your mind—a situation, relationship, or decision.
Step 2: Recognize (60 seconds)
We show you nine embodied metaphor clusters. You select the ones that match—not by thinking, but by recognition.
"Weight pressing down, sinking, drowning, can't catch my breath"
"Want to disappear, feeling small, can't face anyone, shrinking"
"Heart racing, blood running cold, frozen in place"
Your body says "yes" to the ones that fit. Our algorithm translates your selections into your emotional triad.
Step 3: Reveal (60 seconds)
Brief assessment reveals your Core Attachment Style—the survival strategy you've been running your whole life. You answer where this feeling is pointed. You tell us how loud it's running.
Step 4: See (30 seconds)
We show you what you couldn't see before: the weather, the terrain, the direction, the volume—and what it all means together. Personalized insight that feels like recognition, not description.
Slide 7: Same Weather, Different Terrain
Why knowing WHAT you feel isn't enough
Three people select the same metaphors. They're all running Anger / Curiosity / Shame (Dropping). Same triad. Same weather.
But their experiences are completely different—because they're moving through different terrain.
Person A: Heartfelt Defender
"I feel the anger, but I immediately channel it into working harder to prove I'm still valuable. My curiosity obsesses over how to fix what went wrong. The dropping only happens when I'm finally alone and the performance can stop."
What they need: Permission to have messy feelings without losing worthiness.
Person B: Lone Wolf
"I feel the anger, and it confirms my instinct to pull away. My curiosity stays analytical—observing from a safe distance. The dropping feels like retreating into my fortress, where no one can reach me."
What they need: To explore what the withdrawal is protecting.
Person C: Emotional Enthusiast
"I feel the anger intensely, and immediately worry—am I too much? Will this push people away? My curiosity fixates on whether the relationship is okay. The dropping feels like collapsing under the weight of my own emotions."
What they need: Help separating signal from amplification.
Same weather. Different terrain. Different path forward.
Slide 8: The 8 Terrains
Core Attachment Style Archetypes
Secure
Grounded Navigator: Can sit with difficult feelings without being overwhelmed or shutting down. Good boundaries. Emotionally flexible.
Anxious Variants
Emotional Enthusiast: Feels everything deeply. Fears abandonment. Amplifies signals to make sure they're not missed.
Heartfelt Defender: Shows the polished version. Hides the messy parts. Performs competence to stay lovable.
Passionate Pilgrim: Wants to belong completely. Goes all-in. Merges with others to feel whole.
Avoidant Variants
Lone Wolf: Values space and freedom. Self-reliant. Withdraws under stress to stay safe.
Chill Conductor: Lives in the head. Understands but doesn't feel. Intellectualizes to stay in control.
Independent Icon: Prides on not needing anyone. Dismisses vulnerability as weakness.
Disorganized
Mystery Mosaic: Sometimes craves closeness, sometimes pushes away. The attachment system itself is conflicted.
Each archetype transforms how the same emotions are experienced, processed, and expressed. This is why generic advice doesn't work—it ignores the terrain.
